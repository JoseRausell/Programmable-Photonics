Theory

1 · Why a 90° hybrid

A single balanced detector measures only Re(EsELO*), one projection of the field. To recover amplitude and phase we need a second projection shifted by 90°. The hybrid produces four mixtures with LO phases 0°, 180°, 90° and 270°:

E1,2 = ½(Es ± ELO),    E3,4 = ½(Es ± j ejδ ELO)

δ is the phase error of the hybrid (ideally 0). On chip it is usually a 2×4 or 4×4 MMI, or couplers with a 90° phase shifter.

2 · In-phase and quadrature currents

Each pair feeds a balanced photodetector, which removes the |Es|² and |ELO|² terms:

II = R(P1 − P2) = R Re(EsELO*),    IQ = R(P3 − P4) = R Im(EsELO*)
II + jIQ = R √(PsPLO) · s(t) · ej(φs − φLO)

The complex current reproduces the transmitted symbol s(t), amplified by the LO and rotated by the phase difference between the two lasers.

3 · What goes wrong

  • LO phase offset: the whole constellation rotates.
  • Frequency offset Δf between lasers: the constellation spins at Δf, smearing into rings.
  • Laser linewidth Δν: random walk of the phase, variance 2π Δν Ts per symbol.
  • Hybrid phase error δ: I and Q are not orthogonal, the constellation is sheared.
  • I/Q imbalance: different gains stretch one axis.
  • Detector mismatch: the common mode leaks as an offset.
  • Noise: optical noise of the signal, shot and thermal noise blur every point.

4 · Digital signal processing

Modern coherent receivers sample I and Q and correct most impairments digitally: frequency offset estimation, carrier phase recovery (for example the Viterbi–Viterbi 4th-power algorithm for QPSK), I/Q orthogonalization and equalization. Here the ideal carrier recovery removes the exact LO phase, so what remains shows the impairments that phase recovery cannot fix.
