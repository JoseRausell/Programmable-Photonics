Theory: the scattering matrix of a recirculating mesh

1 · Why a transfer matrix is not enough

In a feed-forward mesh light always moves forward, so the circuit is a product of transfer matrices. In a recirculating (2D) mesh every waveguide can be used in both directions and light can go around closed cells: rings, cavities and feedback paths. An output can feed back into an earlier element, so there is no "first" and "last" block to multiply.

Instead every element is described by its scattering matrix S, which relates the waves leaving each port, b, to the waves entering, a: b = S a. The circuit is then a set of linear equations that includes every reflection and every round trip.

2 · The programmable unit cell (PUC)

Each PUC is an MZI with two phase shifters (φ₁, φ₂) and four ports: 0 and 1 on one side, 2 and 3 on the other. Neglecting back-reflections, light only crosses from one side to the other (Sánchez et al., JLT 2022):

SPUC = −j e−jΔ α e−jωτ 00cos θ−sin θ00sin θcos θcos θsin θ00−sin θcos θ00

θ = (φ₁ − φ₂)/2 sets the splitting and Δ = (φ₁ + φ₂)/2 the common phase. α is the PUC loss and τ its delay, the basic unit length (BUL) divided by the group velocity. Three useful states: bar (θ = 0: 0→2, 1→3), cross (θ = π/2: 0→3, 1→2) and tunable coupler (anything in between).

3 · Connecting the cells

Stack the S matrices of all PUCs in one block-diagonal matrix and split the ports into external (E, the mesh I/O) and internal (I, joined at the vertices). The connections impose aI = Γ bI, where the permutation Γ pairs every internal port with its neighbour. Eliminating the internal waves:

Smesh = SEE + SEI Γ (I − SII Γ)−1 SIE

The inverse sums every possible round trip: (I − X)−1 = I + X + X² + … This is where resonances come from. This page solves the sparse linear system (I − SIIΓ) aI = SIE aE exactly at every wavelength, with the unknowns ordered by position so that the matrix is banded.

4 · Inductive and graph-based methods

The same Smesh can be built inductively: start from one PUC and add one PUC at a time, updating the scattering matrix of the growing mesh. The update depends on how the new PUC is attached:

  • S0: through one port (two new external ports);
  • S1: two ports on the same side;
  • S2: one port on each side (closes a cell, adds a feedback loop);
  • S3: three ports.

The graph-based alternative treats ports as vertices and PUC paths as weighted edges. It enumerates the optical paths between an input and an output, multiplying their responses and dropping paths below a power threshold (e.g. −40 dB). It is faster for large meshes and many wavelengths, but approximate for high-Q resonators, which contain infinitely many round trips.

5 · Choosing a topology

Every vertex joins 3 (hexagonal), 4 (square) or 6 (triangular) PUCs. The smallest closed cavity uses 6, 4 or 3 PUCs, which sets the shortest ring delay and hence the largest free spectral range:

FSRcell = λ²ng · Ncell · BUL

Hexagonal meshes need fewer PUCs per vertex and give more reconfiguration options per PUC. Triangular meshes give the shortest cavities, square meshes the simplest routing. In square and triangular meshes (even number of PUCs per vertex) the ports split into two classes: light entering one port can only reach half of the ports (marked “not reachable” in the output list), whereas in a hexagonal mesh every port can reach every other one. All three are simulated here with the same PUC model and the same solver.

6 · Reading the simulation

Power at every port shows where the light leaves the mesh at the chosen wavelength, including the light returning to the input side. Spectrum sweeps the wavelength for the monitored output: a straight route is flat, a closed cell coupled to the route gives periodic notches spaced by FSRcell, and their depth depends on the coupling (κ = sin²θ) and the loss. Group delay −dφ/dω shows the slow light near a resonance.
