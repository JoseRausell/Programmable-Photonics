Theory

1 · Why crossings matter

Photonic circuits are planar: whenever two optical paths must exchange sides, the waveguides cross. Switch fabrics such as Beneš or crossbar networks, and many routing layers, need tens or hundreds of crossings in series, so each one must have a very low insertion loss and crosstalk.

In the intersection the light loses its lateral confinement over a length equal to the width of the other waveguide. There it diffracts like a free beam, and part of it is scattered into the perpendicular waveguide (crosstalk).

2 · Diffraction across the intersection

Modelling the mode as a Gaussian beam of waist w ≈ 0.42 W crossing an unguided length z ≈ W, the field that couples back into the output waveguide is

ηd = [1 + (z / 2zR)²]−1/2,    zR = π n w²λ

A narrow (bare) waveguide diffracts strongly. Widening the waveguide at the centre makes the beam larger and almost collimated, so it crosses with very little loss.

3 · Tapered (MMI-like) crossings

The waveguide is widened from w0 to Wc by tapers of length Lt before the intersection, then narrowed again. The taper must be adiabatic, otherwise power goes into higher-order modes:

Lad ≈ 0.35 n (Wc² − w0²)λ,    ηt ≈ 1 − ½ e−(Lt/Lad)²

For a fixed taper length there is an optimum centre width: too narrow diffracts, too wide is not adiabatic. Practical designs use elliptical or MMI-based tapers that focus the light right at the intersection.

4 · Crosstalk and cascades

Light scattered at the corners of the intersection is launched into the perpendicular waveguide. A wider, better collimated beam has less angular spread, so the crosstalk drops quickly with Wc. In a cascade of N crossings (incoherent approximation):

ILN = N · IL,    XTN ≈ 10 log10(N · 10XT/10)

This is why planar switch architectures without crossings (Spanke–Beneš) or with few of them are attractive.
